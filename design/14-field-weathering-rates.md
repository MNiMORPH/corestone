Written 2026-09-23 in answer to the last open item in design 13: *"What did
White et al. (2001) measure at Panola and Davis Run, over what interval, and is
a 3 m jointed section with rain entering at zero silica the right analogue for a
front under metres of loaded regolith?"*

Read design 11 (the ceiling), 12 (the reference temperature) and 13 (lever by

## 0. The single most important thing for the comparability question

**The model's fracture geometry is not the field's.** In the homogenised
fractured-rock theory of Lebedeva & Brantley, read this session in Brantley &
Lebedeva's valve-model paper (Eq. 9a-9b, `valve.txt` lines 465-485), the
weathering advance rate of a fractured system relates to the unfractured one as

    W_fractured = W_0 / (1 - phi_f) = W_0 (1 + d/delta)^2      (2-D)

with `d` the fracture aperture and `delta` the spacing. Our defaults are
`d = 100 um` and `delta = 1 m`, so `d/delta = 1e-4` and this factor is
**1.0002**.

**Read that factor for exactly what it is.** It is a *volume-fraction* effect,
derived for unidirectional constant downward advection: a fraction `phi_f` of
the rock is inert fracture filling, so there is that much less mineral to
consume. It does **not** contain the block-size effect, which in their model
enters through advection and diffusion *inside* each block and is the part that
a 1 m block does badly. So the correct statement is narrower than "fractures do
nothing": **at realistic joint apertures the geometry of the joint network
itself contributes nothing measurable, and everything then rests on transport
into the block interior** -- which is the `phi = 0.09-0.14` design 13 measured.
Lebedeva & Brantley (2023, `homog2023.txt` l.301) state the corresponding
qualitative result from the other side: *"the weathering advance rate is smaller
for unfractured rocks in comparison to the fractured structures at the same
conditions."*

The field sites that weather fast do not look like that. At Rio Icacos the
bedrock-saprolite interface is a **rindlet zone 0.2-2 m thick made of layers
~2.6 cm apart** (Buss et al. 2008, read this session), i.e. a fracture spacing
**~38x finer than our joints**, and the fractures are *generated by the
weathering itself* rather than inherited. Fletcher et al. (2006, read this
session) show why that matters: with no fracturing their model gives a front
advancing as `t^(1/2)` -- parabolic, diffusion-limited, decelerating -- and it
*cannot* sustain the observed denudation rate; with fracturing the advance rate
is constant in time.

So the honest reading of design 13's measured contacting efficiency
`phi = 0.09-0.14` is not that the model is broken. **It is that a 1 m-jointed
granite block with an impermeable matrix is a different object from a
spheroidally fracturing weathering front, and the field numbers being used as a
target were measured on the second.**

## 1. What is actually measured, and how

Five different quantities travel under the word "rate". They are not
interchangeable and the literature mixes them freely.

| quantity | symbol | method | what boundary is tracked | depth interval |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Regolith / soil production rate** | `P` | in-situ cosmogenic 10Be in saprolite at the base of the mobile soil, or in stream sediment; also U-series disequilibrium on regolith profiles | the soil-saprolite or regolith-bedrock interface | a point interface |
| **Weathering advance rate** | `omega`, `W` | solid-state weathering gradient `b_s` through a profile (White 2002 method), or solute mass balance, or U-series regolith ages | the reaction front of one mineral, or of the rock as a whole | the whole profile, metres to tens of metres |
| **Total denudation rate** | `D` | cosmogenic 10Be in stream sediment; sediment + solute gauging | land surface | catchment |
| **Mineral-specific dissolution rate** | `R` | `R = f * nu * s * b_s * omega`-type gradient relations, normalised to BET or geometric surface area; units mol m-2 s-1 | no boundary -- a rate per unit mineral surface | front thickness |
| **Solute-flux / catchment mass balance** | -- | discharge x concentration, normalised to catchment area, converted to rock equivalent | none; it is a flux, converted to a length rate by assuming steady state | catchment |

Two structural facts that matter for us:

**(a) They are only equal at steady state, and that is an assumption.** Buss et
al. (2008, lines 68-82, read this session): *"If indeed the total mass of
regolith (including saprolite) in a profile is constant in time, then
conservation of mass requires that the rate of formation of regolith equals the
total denudation rate ... Thus, the total denudation rate is commonly equated
with the rate of bedrock transformation to regolith."* Commonly equated. Not
measured to be equal.

**(b) The governing equation for the advance rate is the one our model already
has.** Lebedeva & Brantley (`brantley2020_watertable.txt` line 330, read this
session) give, for unidirectional advective transport,

    W = (C_e - C_R) q / Q_0

with `q` the Darcy velocity, `C_e` the equilibrium concentration of the reacting
mineral, `C_R` the concentration arriving at the front, and `Q_0` the
concentration of the mineral in the rock. That is design 13's
`v = q C_eq phi / N_0` with `phi = 1`. **Our model is not using a different
theory from the field workers. It is using the same one with different
numbers.**

**The spread on granitic/granitoid rock is 2.9 to 334 m/Myr -- two orders of
magnitude -- and most of that is not climate.** The Chilean transect spans a

### 2a. A traceable inflation, and why it should worry us

The 100 m/Myr row above is the same measurement as the 43 m/Myr row. Fletcher
et al. (2006) cite Brown et al. (1995) -- their reference [23], checked against
their reference list -- and render it as *"approximately 1 cm per 100 years"*,
then use `R = 0.01 cm/a` in the model. 43 m/Myr is 0.0043 cm/a. **The secondary
rendering is 2.3x the cited measurement.** Navarre-Sitchler, Brantley & Rother
(2015, RiMG 80 ch.10, p.350, read this session) then cite *Fletcher et al.
(2006)* for "100 mm kyr-1" in a headline comparison against basaltic andesite at
0.24 mm kyr-1.

Nothing here is misconduct; it is ordinary rounding that compounds. But it is
exactly the failure mode that puts "Panola 7, Davis Run 4" in this repository
with no one having read the paper.

## 3. The comparability question

Our model: a 3 m vertical section of jointed granite, 1 m joint spacing, rain
entering the top at zero dissolved silica, no soil, no organic acids, no CO2
enhancement, no physical erosion, no overlying regolith.

### 3.1 Do the classic sites have thick, silica-loaded regolith above the front? Yes -- and it should make them SLOWER, not faster.

Measured regolith thicknesses read this session:

- Rio Icacos: **2-8 m of saprolite plus 0.5-1 m of soil** (Buss et al. 2008
  l.176), or **5-9 m augerable regolith** (Buss et al. 2017 l.723).
- Bisley (volcaniclastic): 9-16 m.
- Sri Lanka: **>10 m** of clay-rich saprolite (Hewawasam et al. 2013,
  abstract).
- Virginia Piedmont metagranite: regolith and plagioclase front **20x thicker
  than on a nearby diabase**, with weathered rock sampled at 20 m depth
  (Navarre-Sitchler et al. 2015 p.341, reporting Bazilevskaya et al. 2013).
- Conventwald: regolith-bedrock interface at 7 m.

And their porewater is loaded. Buss et al. 2017 Table 4 (read as raw table text,
not summarised) gives pore water at the Bisley B1R ridgetop, 0.15 to 16 m depth:
**Si = 240, 180, 166, 144, 128, 121, 131, 174, 170, 170, 180, 191, 210 uM**, at
pH 4.4-4.8. Converted by me, 121-240 uM Si is **7.3-14.4 mg/L as SiO2**, i.e.
1.2 to 2.4x quartz saturation. (That profile is volcaniclastic, not granitic;
the granitic LG1 pore waters in the same study come from White et al. 1998,
which I did not obtain.)

**Now apply `W = (C_e - C_R) q / Q_0`.** Our model sets `C_R = 0`. The field
sets `C_R` to whatever the overlying regolith delivers, which is **positive**.
Zero inlet concentration is the *generous* boundary condition. **The regolith
above the front cannot explain why the field is faster than us; it works the
other way.** Whatever the answer is, it is in `q`, in `C_e`, or in contacting --
which is what design 13 measured.

### 3.2 Is fracture control the right idealisation for these sites?

For the tropical granitoid sites, **yes -- but at centimetre spacing, generated
by the reaction.** Navarre-Sitchler et al. (2015, p.350) attribute the
two-order-of-magnitude gap between a non-fractured basaltic andesite
(0.24 mm kyr-1) and the spheroidally weathered quartz diorite (100 mm kyr-1)
squarely to transport mechanism: *"The difference in solute transport through
the reaction front -- diffusion for the non-fractured basaltic andesite versus
advection in the spheroidally weathered felsic rock -- is considered the
explanation for the orders of magnitude difference in weathering advance
rates."*

For the Virginia Piedmont metagranite, the same argument: *"the greater
thickness of the regolith and the reaction front on the metagranite compared to
the diabase was due to differences in solute transport during early weathering:
WIF [weathering-induced fracturing] allowed solute transport by advection that
in turn led to thicker regolith. In contrast, on the nearby diabase ...
microfracturing was not observed and solute transport was limited to diffusive
processes."* (p.341.)

For Santa Cruz, **no** -- that is an unconsolidated granitic beach-sand terrace,
matrix-controlled by construction, and Maher et al. (2009) model it as a porous
medium with geometric surface areas.

So the field literature agrees with the model's *premise* (fractures set the
rate) and disagrees with the model's *geometry* (1 m spacing, inherited,
static).

### 3.3 Does the field rate depend on regolith thickness? Yes, explicitly.

Fletcher et al. (2006, lines 725-760, read this session) make the regolith
thickness the controlling variable. They let the O2 concentration at the front
fall linearly with soil+saprolite thickness `z`, `c_R = c_atm + b z` with `b`
negative, and derive `z_ss = (1/b)[(R/q)^5 - c_atm]`. Their own summary:
*"as erosion rate increases at constant atmospheric O2, the soil thickness at
steady state decreases ... as R increases and z_ss decreases, c_R increases,
causing a higher rate of weathering"*, and, simulating a halving of `c_R`,
*"W and t_crack both increase, documenting a decrease in weathering advance
rate."*

Buss et al. 2008 (l.96) restate it: *"deeper soils would have slower weathering
advance rates than shallower soils."*

Separately, at the *soil* (mobile-layer) scale, the production function
`P = P_0 e^(-bh)(1 + ch)` (Brantley et al. 2016, Eq. 10, read this session)
reduces to a plain exponential for `c = 0` and is humped for `c > 0`; the review
reports that **exponential, humped and flat forms have all been observed**
(Heimsath et al. 1999, 2000; Riggins et al. 2011; Wilkinson et al. 2005; Dixon
et al. 2009 as cited there).

**Consequence for us: a single field number is not a property of the rock.** It
is a property of the rock *at that regolith thickness and that erosion rate*.
Comparing a bare 3 m section against it is comparing against a point on a curve
whose position we did not specify.

### 3.4 Soil CO2 and organic acids

What I could verify:

- **Soil pCO2 at Santa Cruz was measured at 0.1 bar** (Maher et al. 2009,
  section 1.2, read this session) -- about 250x the atmospheric partial
  pressure. Their sensitivity test spans an order of magnitude either side, and
  they find *"the effect of increased PCO2 is [to] increase the weathering
  advance rate similar to the effects observed for increased flow"*, but that
  the fitted **primary-mineral** rate constants are insensitive to it; the
  uncertainty lands on the secondary (kaolinite) precipitation rate instead.
- Bisley pore waters sit at **pH 4.4-5.7** (Buss et al. 2017 Table 4, raw
  table text).

What the pH actually buys, computed by me from Palandri & Kharaka (2004)
Table 13, albite row read directly from the extracted PDF text
(`-10.16 / 65.0 / 0.457` acid, `-12.56 / 69.8` neutral,
`-15.60 / 71.0 / -0.572` base):

    pH 8.0   R = 9.75e-12 mol m-2 s-1
    pH 7.0   R = 2.85e-12
    pH 6.0   R = 1.08e-12
    pH 5.0   R = 8.16e-13
    pH 4.5   R = 9.77e-13
    pH 4.0   R = 1.35e-12
    minimum near pH 5.2

**On this parameterisation, acidifying albite from pH 7 to pH 5 makes it 3.4x
SLOWER, not faster.** The three-mechanism law has its minimum right where soil
water sits. I record this because it is the opposite of the intuition that "soil
CO2 and organic acids are the missing multiplier", and because it is a
calculation anyone can redo from the file. Caveats it deserves: it is far-from-
equilibrium, it ignores organic-ligand complexation entirely, and P&K's base
term is fitted across hydrothermal temperatures. **It is not a licence to say
acidity does not matter; it is a statement that the acid lever is not worth a
factor of 20, and that nobody should put one in the model on the strength of a
hunch.**

Organic acids: I found no quantified field multiplier I could read. Navarre-
Sitchler et al. (2015, p.340) note only that *"Weathering in the outer rindlets
is thought to be enhanced by the presence of Fe-oxidizing organisms that further
accelerate weathering by production of organic acids that mobilize Fe and Al"* --
qualitative, and about Fe and Al rather than Si.

### 3.5 Is there a measured site that is honestly our analogue?

**No, and that is the finding.** Working down the list:

- **Rio Icacos** is fracture-controlled granitoid, which is what we want, but it
  carries 3-9 m of regolith, sits under an infiltration rate of **3.2 m/yr**
  (site LG1, Buss et al. 2017 Table 6, read this session) against our 0.30, and
  its fracture spacing at the front is 2.6 cm against our 1 m.
- **Santa Cruz** has the flux we could match (fitted infiltration
  **0.058 m/yr**, Maher et al. 2009 Table 4 footnote f) but it is
  unconsolidated sand with no fractures at all.
- **Sri Lanka** is the closest in spirit -- hard, tightly interlocked crystalline
  rock, slow erosion, spheroidal weathering with corestones -- and gives
  **6-14 m/Myr**. But it carries >10 m of saprolite and 2000 mm/yr of rain.
- **Bare, thinly mantled, jointed granite with a measured front rate**: I did not
  find one. Everything measured has regolith on it, because the methods
  (cosmogenic nuclides at an interface, solid-state gradients, U-series on
  regolith) require regolith to exist.

The infiltration spread alone is instructive: **0.058 m/yr (Santa Cruz) to
5.3 m/yr (Bisley), a factor of 91**, all of it multiplying `W` linearly through
`W = (C_e - C_R) q / Q_0`. Our 0.30 m/yr is a choice, and design 12's supply
ceiling of 3.41 m/Myr scales with it: at Rio Icacos's 3.2 m/yr the same identity
gives 36 m/Myr. (Arithmetic mine, on the model's own published identity.)

## 4. The lab-field discrepancy

This is the part that makes 8-35x unremarkable.

**Measured, for plagioclase, all read this session in White & Brantley (2003),
Chemical Geology 202:479-506 (local copy `wb2003.pdf`, Table 4 extracted as raw
text and read row by row):**

| environment | log R (mol m-2 s-1) | source cited in that table |
|---|---|---|
| Davis Run saprolite, VA -- solids | **-16.4** | White et al. 2001 |
| China Hat soil, Merced CA | -16.3 | White et al. 1996 |
| Panola bedrock, GA -- solids | **-15.7** | White et al. 2001 |
| Merced soils (range) | -15.1 to -16.3 | White et al. 1996 |
| Columns, *weathered* Panola Granite | -14.9 | that paper |
| Columns, *fresh* Panola Granite | -13.2 | that paper |
| Fluidized-bed / flow-through reactors, fresh mineral | -10.6 to -12.1 | various |

And in the text (p.487): the field rate for plagioclase in the Panola
weathering profile calculated by White et al. (2001) is **2.8e-16 mol m-2 s-1**,
which they describe as *"an order of magnitude slower than for the experimental
rate for the weathered Panola Granite (2.2e-15) and more than two orders of
magnitude slower than the fresh granite (7.0e-14)."*

Ratios computed by me from those numbers: **250x** (Panola field vs fresh
column), **7.9x** (field vs weathered column), and **2e4** (Davis Run saprolite
vs a fluidized-bed albite reactor).

Their generalisation, from a tabulation spanning six orders of magnitude in
duration (4 days to 3 Myr):

    R = 3.1e-13 * t^(-0.61)        R in mol m-2 s-1, t in years

**Current explanations, quantified where I could:**

- **Approach to equilibrium and the form of the affinity term.** Maher et al.
  (2009), read this session: a linear-TST fit to the Santa Cruz profile gives
  rate constants *"approximately 2 orders of magnitude slower than the
  experimental values"*, but the Al-inhibition and parallel (close-to-
  equilibrium) rate laws *"resulted in rate constants that are comparable to
  laboratory values."* Their conclusion: *"Experimental and field rates,
  therefore, can be reconciled at the Santa Cruz site."* Their Table 4 makes it
  concrete -- White et al. (2008)'s field weathering-gradient value is
  log R = -15.3 for both albite and K-feldspar, against a temperature-corrected
  lab reference of -13.1 at 12 C. That is **158x**, computed by me.
- **Intrinsic surface area growth with time** accounts for *"a third of the
  exponential decrease"* (White & Brantley 2003, abstract).
- **Extrinsic controls**: *"low permeability, high mineral/fluid ratios and
  increased solute concentrations, produce weathering reactions close to
  thermodynamic equilibrium under field conditions"* (same abstract).
- Maher et al. list, with citations, the full menu: intrinsic surface
  reactivity, reactive surface area, **transport limitation into low-permeability
  zones**, slow secondary-mineral precipitation, and transport rather than
  interface control.

**What this means for us.** A field-to-lab gap of **2 to 4 orders of magnitude**
is the normal condition of this subject, and the leading explanation for it is
*transport limitation and proximity to equilibrium* -- which is precisely the
`phi = 0.09-0.14` that design 13 measured and called the defect. **An 8-35x
model-field discrepancy is small by the standards of this literature.** It is
about the size of a rate-law-form choice.

## 5. Temperature and climate

**Lab activation energies, read directly from the extracted text of Palandri &
Kharaka (2004) Table 13, line 1378-1379:**

    Albite       acid Ea 65.0 kJ/mol   neutral 69.8   base 71.0
    Oligoclase   acid Ea 65.0          neutral 69.8   (no base term)

Maher et al. (2009) use 69.8 kJ/mol for albite (Hellmann & Tisserand 2006),
38 kJ/mol for K-feldspar and 22.2 for kaolinite (both P&K).

**Field apparent activation energies are lower, and the reason is structural.**
Fletcher et al. (2006), lines 524-530, read this session:

> *"the apparent activation energy of weathering rate advance for this type
> model would be expected to be significantly smaller than the activation energy
> for mineral dissolution. Such a discrepancy has been observed by researchers
> reporting apparent activation energies that are in some cases 2-4 times lower
> for field observations of weathering as compared to silicate weathering rates
> in the laboratory [3]."*

Their reference [3], checked in their reference list, is Riebe, Kirchner &
Finkel (2004) EPSL 224:547-562, *Erosional and climatic effects on long-term
chemical weathering rates in granitic landscapes spanning diverse climate
regimes* -- **which I could not obtain** (closed access). So "2-4x lower" is a
statement I read in Fletcher, about Riebe, and have not checked against Riebe.

The mechanism Fletcher gives is worth keeping: in their model the dependence on
the lumped **transport** parameter is much stronger than on the lumped
**kinetic** parameter, so a temperature dependence measured on the front
inherits the weak one.

**How much of the tropical-temperate spread is temperature vs runoff?** The one
clean single-lithology test I could read says **neither dominates.** Oeser & von
Blanckenburg (2020), four granitoid sites, MAP 10 to 1084 mm/yr, MAT 18.1 to
14.1 C:

> *"neither MAP nor NPP exerts control over weathering at all"* (line 1067),
> and *"weathering rate does not correlate with NPP or MAP"* (1061-1062).

Their denudation rates -- 2.9, 3.4, 74, 10 m/Myr converted by me -- are not
monotonic in rainfall. The largest value sits at the *middle* site.

Against that, the tropical granitoid sites (Rio Icacos 43-58 m/Myr, MAT ~22 C by
common knowledge but **not read by me**) are 4-15x the two cold temperate
paragneiss sites (Conventwald 46, Mitterfels 21 m/Myr, MAT 6.8 and 5.5 C) --
except that Conventwald at 6.8 C is *as fast as* Rio Icacos. **The
temperature signal does not survive contact with the site-to-site scatter.**

## 7. What this means for the comparison

**Is 4-7 m/Myr the right target for this model? No. Three separate reasons, in
descending order of confidence.**

**1. The number is unsourced.** Nobody in this project has read White et al.
(2001), and I could not. It sits in `weathering.py` line 203, in two test
docstrings and on the teaching page as a bare pair of integers with no quantity,
no method and no depth interval attached. Given design 08's record of a
fabricated citation in this same repository, and given section 2a's demonstration
that a factor of 2.3 can enter the secondary literature on a rounding,
**the first action is to stop asserting it, not to chase it with the model.**

**2. Even if the number is right, it is not a rate our model computes.** The
field `omega` is the velocity of a *reaction front propagating into fresh rock
beneath metres of regolith at a steady erosion rate*. Design 13 already
established that the model has no front: at t90 the whole section is uniformly
part-reacted and `D/t90` rises with the depth drawn. **Comparing a
no-front quantity to a front velocity is the same class of error design 13
caught once already.**

**3. The comparison is not like-for-like in three measured respects, and they
do not all push the same way.**

| | field | model | direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| inlet concentration `C_R` | loaded (7-14 mg/L SiO2 measured at Bisley) | **zero** | model is FAVOURED |
| infiltration `q` | 0.058 to 5.3 m/yr measured | 0.30 m/yr | model is mid-range; Rio Icacos is 10.7x |
| fracture spacing at the front | ~2.6 cm (Rio Icacos rindlets) | **1 m** | model is 38x coarser |
| fracture volume-fraction term `(1+d/delta)^2` | small at the joint scale too; the gain is in block size | **1.0002** | neither side gains from joint geometry alone |
| regolith above | 3 to 20 m | none | field front is O2-starved, hence SLOWER per Fletcher |
| lab-to-field rate gap in the literature | 2-4 orders of magnitude | 8-35x | model gap is SMALL by comparison |

**The verdict.** The model is not obviously too slow. It is a **bare, coarsely
jointed, matrix-limited granite block**, and the literature's own theory
(`W_fractured = W_0 (1 + d/delta)^2`) says such a block should advance at close
to the unfractured rate. The 4-100 m/Myr field numbers come from systems where
the weathering *makes its own fractures at centimetre spacing*, and Fletcher et
al. (2006) show that without that mechanism the front is parabolic and cannot
keep up with denudation at all. **Our model is reproducing the no-fracture case
faithfully and being scored against the with-fracture case.**

**What to do instead of tuning.** Three options, costed:

1. **Drop the field comparison from the validation section and say why.**
   Free. Honest. Loses the only external check the page has.
2. **Re-target on a quantity the model actually computes.** The effluent silica
   concentration is already the discriminator design 13 identified, and it has
   *measured* field values: 121-240 uM Si at a tropical ridgetop (Buss et al.
   2017 Table 4), 14-25 mg/L SiO2 in granitic groundwaters (design 11). The
   model's 0.47 mg/L at the defaults is 15-50x under. **This is a real, sourced
   discrepancy and it does not depend on any front-rate claim.**
3. **Make the model's joint spacing a stated limitation rather than a
   parameter to tune.** If the honest analogue requires reaction-generated
   fracturing at centimetre spacing, then 1 m joints is a *scope decision*, and
   design 02's teaching scope is where it belongs -- not in a calibration.

**What would actually settle it** is Hayes et al. (2019) and Bazilevskaya et al.
(2013), in that order. Both are about granitic weathering fronts in contrasting
climates and one of them is about the Virginia Piedmont site whose number we are
quoting. Neither is reachable from this machine.
